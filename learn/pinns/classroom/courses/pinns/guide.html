1.1 / One central question: Why not just fit observations?

Introduce and explain: Before defining the mathematics, let us start with the practical question that makes physical constraints useful when measurements are sparse. Physics-informed neural networks, or PINNs, combine a function model with physical constraints. Imagine a heated rod with only a few temperature sensors. The left part of our schematic represents measurements, and the right part represents information supplied by an equation. We want a continuous temperature field between sensors, rather than an unconstrained collection of guesses.

Connect, check and continue: This is a modelling strategy, not a promise to outperform a numerical solver. Our route is to formulate one heat problem, turn its equation into a differentiable residual, train a network, and then test both accurate and misleading answers. Later we will infer an unknown coefficient. Keep asking what information selects the solution and what evidence would justify trusting it.

中文讲稿

在写数学之前，先看一个实际问题：测量稀疏时，为什么还需要物理约束。 物理信息神经网络，简称 PINNs，把函数模型和物理约束结合起来。设想受热细杆只有少量温度传感器，左边是测量，右边是方程提供的信息。我们希望得到传感器之间的连续温度场，而不是一组不受约束的猜测。

1.2 / One central question: What does the heat equation connect?

ut=αuxx,x,t∈[0,1]u_t=\alpha u_{xx},\quad x,t\in[0,1]ut​=αuxx​,x,t∈[0,1]

Introduce and explain: We have motivated combining measurements with physics, so we now need a precise evolution law that tells our temperature model what physically consistent behaviour means. The heat equation is a partial differential equation, or PDE. Here x is position, t is time, and u of x and t is temperature relative to a reference level. Our teaching coordinates and temperature are dimensionless. Alpha is the effective diffusion coefficient, set to zero point one in the forward experiment. A subscript t means a first time derivative, and x x means a second spatial derivative. The coloured rod and the curve below it show the same temperature profile evolving from a warm middle with both ends held cold. Orange means warmer, blue means colder, and the dashed curve preserves the initial sine profile for comparison. Watch the middle cool as heat flows toward the cold ends, while the solid temperature curve decays without changing its sine shape.

Connect, check and continue: The two schematic quantities explain the equation together: local temperature changes in proportion to spatial curvature. Positive alpha smooths a profile. A straight spatial profile has zero curvature and therefore no local change under this equation. But the evolution law does not specify how the rod starts or how its ends are held. Those conditions select a particular experiment.

1.3 / One central question: Conditions select the particular solution

u(x,0)=sin⁡(πx),u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0u(x,0)=\sin(\pi x),\quad u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0u(x,0)=sin(πx),u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0

Introduce and explain: The heat equation tells our model how temperature evolves, but it cannot select the starting profile or endpoint temperatures, which is why we now introduce initial and boundary conditions. The first expression fixes the initial temperature profile at time zero. Pi is the circle constant and x remains the position. The second expression fixes both endpoints at zero for every time, a Dirichlet boundary condition. In space-time these are distinct sets: one initial line and two boundary lines, represented by the two schematic parts.

Connect, check and continue: Two functions can satisfy the same heat equation while describing different starting profiles. They solve different experiments even if both have zero interior residual. We therefore need an equation constraint, an initial constraint and a boundary constraint. Keep their roles separate, because each will become its own training term. Next we choose a case with an exact reference solution so our numerical claims can be checked.

中文讲稿

1.4 / One central question: Why keep an exact reference solution?

u∗(x,t)=e−απ2tsin⁡(πx)u^*(x,t)=e^{-\alpha\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)u∗(x,t)=e−απ2tsin(πx)

Introduce and explain: We have specified the equation and its conditions, and an exact solution will now give us a way to check whether a trained model actually solves that particular problem. The star marks a reference solution, not a learned parameter. The sine factor gives spatial shape and the exponential factor gives temporal decay. Differentiating in time produces minus alpha pi squared, while two spatial derivatives of the sine produce minus pi squared. Thus the two sides of the heat equation agree, directly connecting each factor in the schematic to the equation.

Connect, check and continue: At time zero the exponential is one, and the sine vanishes at both endpoints, so the conditions also hold. During forward training we withhold these interior reference values. Afterwards they provide an independent test of solution error. That separation matters: minimising the training objective and recovering the correct physical field are related but different achievements.

中文讲稿

方程和条件已经确定，接下来需要一个解析解，用来核查训练后的模型是否真正解对了这个具体问题。 星号表示参考解，不是学习参数。正弦因子给出空间形状，指数因子给出时间衰减。时间求导产生负 alpha pi 平方，二次空间求导产生负 pi 平方，所以热方程两边一致。

1.5 / One central question: Forward and inverse problems

α→uuobs→α\alpha\to u\qquad u_{\rm obs}\to\alphaα→uuobs​→α

Introduce and explain: The exact solution shows how diffusivity controls temperature, which lets us distinguish predicting a field from a known coefficient and inferring an unknown coefficient from measurements. In a forward problem alpha and the conditions are known, and the temperature field is unknown. In an inverse problem, u subscript obs denotes observed temperatures, and alpha becomes an additional unknown. The arrows describe inference, not a guarantee that any small dataset identifies the coefficient. The same residual machinery can support both tasks.

Connect, check and continue: For example, observations only at time zero do not identify alpha in our example because the prescribed initial sine does not depend on it. Positive-time measurements contain decay information, although noise can weaken the inference. We will solve the forward case first and return to this information question before jointly fitting a field and coefficient.

中文讲稿

解析解展示了扩散系数怎样影响温度，因此现在可以区分两种任务：已知系数预测场，以及通过测量反推未知系数。 正问题已知 alpha 和条件，求未知温度场。反问题的 u 下标 obs 表示观测温度，alpha 也成为未知量。箭头代表推断方向，不保证任意少量观测都能识别系数。两类任务可以共享残差结构。

1.6 / One central question: The network represents a continuous field

(x,t)↦uθ(x,t)(x,t)\mapsto u_\theta(x,t)(x,t)↦uθ​(x,t)

Introduce and explain: We now know which quantities each task must recover, so the next step is to choose how a neural network will represent the unknown temperature field. Theta denotes all trainable weights and biases. The subscript on u labels a neural prediction rather than the exact solution. One coordinate pair produces one predicted temperature, and many queries describe a continuous field. All these predictions share theta, so this is not a separate free value at each training location.

Connect, check and continue: Shared parameters allow a constraint at one location to influence other predictions through an update. They also make training a coupled optimisation problem. We use smooth hyperbolic-tangent activations because the residual requires second coordinate derivatives. Expressive function classes do not guarantee successful optimisation, a distinction we will revisit when the network produces an apparently plausible but incorrect field.

1.7 / One central question: Turn the equation into local mismatch

rθ=∂tuθ−α∂xxuθr_\theta=\partial_tu_\theta-\alpha\partial_{xx}u_\thetarθ​=∂t​uθ​−α∂xx​uθ​

Introduce and explain: The network gives us a temperature prediction, but we still need a computable test of whether that prediction obeys the heat equation, so we construct the residual. The letter r denotes a residual: substitute the predicted field into the heat equation and move all terms to one side. Partial t and partial x x are coordinate derivatives, not updates of theta. Residual zero means predicted time change agrees with alpha times predicted curvature at that queried location.

Connect, check and continue: The schematic therefore interprets the formula as a test of local consistency. It does not test whether we chose the correct initial state. Nor does a small residual averaged over finitely many points prove that it is small everywhere. We now have the central PINN mechanism, but the next prediction question exposes exactly why residual information must be combined with conditions.

h=tanh⁡(Wq+b),q=(x,t)Th=\tanh(Wq+b),\quad q=(x,t)^\mathsf{T}h=tanh(Wq+b),q=(x,t)T

Introduce and explain: The zero-field example showed why we need more than an equation residual, and before assembling all the losses we must choose a network whose coordinate derivatives can actually be computed. Here q is the column vector of coordinates, h is a hidden-layer output, W is a weight matrix, and b is a bias vector. The superscript T denotes transpose, turning the written coordinate pair into a column. The schematic follows the same mapping as the equation: a linear combination of coordinates is passed through the smooth hyperbolic tangent.

Connect, check and continue: We need this smoothness because the heat residual contains a second spatial derivative. A standard ReLU network is piecewise linear in its inputs, so its second derivative is zero almost everywhere and problematic at the kinks for this strong-form residual. That does not prohibit every piecewise-linear scientific model, but it motivates a smooth choice here. Next we see how a computational graph supplies derivatives without finite differences.

2.2 / One central question: Automatic differentiation follows the chain rule

Introduce and explain: A smooth network makes the required derivatives meaningful, and automatic differentiation now supplies a practical way to evaluate them through the computation graph. The symbols d u over d h and d h over d x describe sensitivities along two edges of a computation. Here we temporarily consider a scalar chain to make the rule visible. Automatic differentiation applies this chain rule to recorded elementary operations, evaluating derivatives of the represented numerical function rather than perturbing the coordinate by a small step.

Connect, check and continue: It is not symbolic algebra that necessarily produces a human-readable expression, and it is not immune to floating-point error. 2.3 / One central question: Coordinate derivatives are not parameter updates

Introduce and explain: We have seen how the chain rule computes derivatives, but we must separate derivatives with respect to coordinates from gradients that update the network parameters. Partial x u theta tells us how the current predicted field changes as position changes while theta is held fixed. Nabla theta L is the gradient of the training loss with respect to all trainable weights and biases. L denotes the objective we will construct shortly. The two schematic roles prevent a common confusion: evaluating a PDE derivative is not itself a learning step.

Connect, check and continue: First the network and coordinate derivatives form the residual and the scalar loss. Then backpropagation differentiates that loss with respect to theta, and the optimiser updates theta. Consequently the coordinate-derivative operations must remain differentiable with respect to parameters. In PyTorch, create graph equals true provides the graph needed to differentiate through these derivatives during training.

2.4 / One central question: Two derivative calls build the heat residual

ut, ux ⟶ uxx ⟶ rθu_t,\ u_x\ \longrightarrow\ u_{xx}\ \longrightarrow\ r_\thetaut​, ux​ ⟶ uxx​ ⟶ rθ​

Introduce and explain: With the roles of coordinate and parameter derivatives separated, we can now translate the heat residual into tensor operations without confusing equation evaluation with learning. In our implementation the input tensor has two columns in the fixed order x then t. One gradient call returns both u x and u t, and a second call differentiates u x with respect to the coordinates and keeps its x component. Subtracting alpha times u x x from u t gives the same residual we derived on the previous chapter, now as executable tensor operations.

Connect, check and continue: The convenient gradient of summed outputs gives per-point coordinate derivatives only because our model processes input rows independently. Layers that couple samples require more careful Jacobian treatment. We explicitly enable gradients on input coordinates and use ones like u as the vector argument to autograd. 6.2 / One central question: When is a PINN worth comparing with a mesh solver?

Introduce and explain: Extending the equation changes the numerical challenge, but evaluating a neural method also requires a fair comparison with conventional solvers. A mesh method represents a field on a numerical discretisation, whereas our coordinate network represents a differentiable function with shared parameters. This difference alone establishes neither superiority nor inferiority. For a simple one-dimensional forward heat problem, a conventional solver can be an excellent and efficient baseline. PINNs can be attractive when data fusion or inverse calibration is central, but that advantage must be demonstrated for the task.

Connect, check and continue: Compare physical accuracy at matched conditions and report training cost, inference cost and information supplied to each method. Include implementation maturity and robustness, not just a single wall-clock number. Differentiability and convenient sensor integration are capabilities; they are not substitutes for reliable numerical evidence. A research claim should make clear which capability its experiment actually evaluates.

Introduce and explain: Comparing representations is useful only when the learning tasks match, so we now distinguish fitting one physical instance from learning a family of solution functions. Here g denotes an input function, such as an initial profile, and u subscript g denotes the corresponding solution. An operator-learning model aims to learn this function-to-function mapping across many instances. Our earlier PINN instead fits coordinate-to-temperature values for one specified set of conditions. Connect, check and continue: DeepONet and Fourier neural operators are examples of operator-learning approaches, and physical constraints can also be incorporated into such models. Identify whether your intended use is one costly inverse fit, repeated forward queries for one field, or fast solutions across a parameter or function family.

6.4 / One central question: Turn a research question into a controlled experiment

Introduce and explain: With the task and baseline clearly defined, we can now turn a proposed improvement into a hypothesis that a controlled experiment could disprove. A useful hypothesis predicts an outcome that could fail. For example, suppose poor initial matching is caused by gradient imbalance rather than missing information. Define one intervention, keep the problem and sampling budget fixed, and choose independent field and condition errors as outcomes. The arrows describe the experiment design, not a guaranteed improvement.

Connect, check and continue: Run multiple seeds and document failures as well as successes. Compare at a common computational budget when claiming efficiency, and report settings needed to reproduce every curve. If several mechanisms change at once, the result may demonstrate a working recipe but not the contribution of one component. This distinction is central when reading recent PINN papers critically.

6.5 / One central question: How should you audit a PINN paper?

Introduce and explain: Designing a falsifiable experiment also tells us what evidence to seek in a paper, so we now apply the same questions to critical reading. Begin by identifying the exact equation, domain, conditions, data access and unknowns. Then inspect how derivatives, constraints and sampling are implemented. Ask which reference solution or baseline supports the error claim, whether evaluation points differ from training points, and whether hyperparameter selection used information unavailable in the intended application.

Connect, check and continue: Next examine runtime definitions, seeds, ablations, parameter regimes and failure cases. Separate a new representation, a training heuristic and a theoretical guarantee; they demand different evidence. Our reading list includes the original formulation, training guidance and explicit failure analysis so you can compare claims rather than relying on a single successful example. Use open author manuscripts and their documented code where available.

中文讲稿

设计可证伪实验也告诉我们阅读论文时应寻找什么证据，因此现在把相同问题用于批判性阅读。 先找出方程、区域、条件、可用数据和未知量，再检查导数、约束与采样实现。误差由什么参考支持，评估点是否独立，超参数选择是否使用了实际任务中不可获得的信息？

Sources and further reading

  1. Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis, Physics Informed Deep Learning (2017), Parts I and II

    Open author exposition and code for differentiable solution networks, PDE residuals, initial/boundary observations, and forward/inverse modelling. Author manuscripts: https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10561 and https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10566 .

  2. Wang, Sankaran, Wang & Perdikaris, An Expert’s Guide to Training PINNs (2023)

    Open manuscript covering nondimensionalization, architecture, loss balancing, temporal causality, curriculum, sampling and boundary constraints. Practical methods are problem-dependent and are not universal convergence guarantees.

  3. Krishnapriyan et al., Characterizing Possible Failure Modes in PINNs (2021)

    Open manuscript demonstrating optimisation failures for convection, reaction and reaction-diffusion problems and investigating curriculum and sequential-in-time training. Expressive networks need not be easy to optimize.

  4. PyTorch official autograd tutorial

    Official graph-based automatic differentiation documentation. The independent lab uses torch.autograd.grad(create_graph=True) for coordinate derivatives that remain differentiable with respect to network weights.

  5. DeepXDE official heat-equation example

    Official heat example with sinusoidal initial data and zero Dirichlet boundaries. This course independently chooses a dimensionless unit domain, alpha=0.1 and exp(-alpha*pi^2*t)*sin(pi*x), rather than reproducing published accuracy claims.

  6. Independent locally executed PINN experiment

    Synthetic dimensionless heat problem: alpha=0.1, sine initial data, zero boundary values. Small smooth PyTorch networks trained on CPU with seed 7 in forward, inverse and omitted-initial-condition configurations. Held-out relative L2 and independent residual checks are logged. Inverse observations include Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.005. These are teaching runs, not paper benchmarks.

  7. Wang, Teng & Perdikaris, Understanding and mitigating gradient pathologies in PINNs (2020)

    Primary study of imbalanced back-propagated gradients in composite PINN objectives, with adaptive training strategies. Used for the diagnosis of conflicting loss components, not as a guarantee that a specific weighting rule always works.

  8. Wang, Yu & Perdikaris, When and why PINNs fail to train (2020)

    Primary neural-tangent-kernel analysis of discrepant convergence rates and training difficulties, under its stated assumptions. Its limiting analysis is not an exact description of every finite nonlinear network.

  9. Wang, Sankaran & Perdikaris, Respecting causality is all you need for training PINNs (2022)

    Primary temporal-training study motivating the need to respect causal evolution in difficult time-dependent problems. Its title should not be interpreted as a universal guarantee across all PDEs.

  10. Li et al., Fourier Neural Operator (2020); Lu et al., DeepONet (2019)

    Primary operator-learning papers: https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08895 and https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.03193 . Learning a mapping between functions across a problem family is distinct from fitting one coordinate network to one fixed PDE instance.
